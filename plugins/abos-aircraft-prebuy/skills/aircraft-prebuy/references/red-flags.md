# Red flags, deal-breakers and fraud patterns

## Fraud patterns in aircraft sales

Aircraft listings, including those shared in social media groups, attract scams. Check these before anything else when money is involved.

- **Price far below comparable aircraft**, often with a story: estate sale, divorce, deployment overseas, urgent relocation.
- **Seller cannot show the aircraft** in person or on a live video call, or is "abroad" and will "ship" it.
- **Deposit or payment demanded before inspection**, especially by wire to a personal account, cryptocurrency, gift cards or a money-transfer service.
- **An escrow company the seller chooses** that the buyer has never heard of, with a new website, free email address or bank account in a different name or country. Fake escrow sites are common.
- **Ownership does not match.** The seller's name differs from the registered owner and there is no verifiable authority to sell.
- **Recycled listings.** Photos, text or registration marks copied from another listing or an older sale; a reverse image search can reveal this.
- **Changed payment instructions** by email or message late in the deal, which is typical of email account takeover.
- **Pressure tactics**: "another buyer is ready", "price valid today only".

Advice when any of these appear:

1. Do not send money.
2. Verify the registered owner in the official register and speak to them using contact details found independently.
3. Use only a recognised aviation escrow or title company that the buyer selects and verifies, and confirm bank details by phone using a number found independently.
4. See the aircraft, or have the inspector see it, and match the data plate to the registration and records.
5. If fraud is likely, tell the user to report it to the platform and, if money was sent, to their bank and the police immediately.

## Deal-breakers until resolved

- No logbooks or missing engine logbooks
- Seller refuses an independent pre-buy inspection or refuses access to records
- Unrepaired or undocumented substantial damage
- Prop strike or sudden engine stoppage without the manufacturer-required inspection
- Significant structural corrosion, especially on primary structure such as spars and attach fittings
- Title problems: unreleased liens, disputed ownership, missing bills of sale in the chain of title
- Data plate, serial numbers or engine and propeller identity that do not match the records
- Non-compliance with recurring ADs that requires costly work the price does not reflect
- Installed modifications with no approval paperwork, particularly where the buyer will register the aircraft in another country

## Signs of hidden damage history

- Logbook entries for skin, spar, rib, firewall or gear replacement with no clear explanation
- Wings, tail or engine removed and reinstalled outside a normal overhaul
- Propeller or engine replaced well before their due time
- Fresh paint on part of the aircraft only, colour mismatches, uneven rivet lines or filler under the paint
- Doors, cowlings or control surfaces that do not fit or align well
- Weight and balance revised without a recorded alteration
- A long gap in the records followed by extensive work

## Other high-risk conditions

- Long-term storage without engine preservation: internal corrosion risk
- Very low annual utilisation over many years
- Coastal, humid or flood exposure
- High-cycle or high-stress history: training, towing, skydiving, aerial work, agricultural use
- Engine past manufacturer's recommended TBO in hours or calendar years with no supporting condition data
- Frequent changes of ownership in a short time
- Obsolete avionics or autopilot with poor parts support
