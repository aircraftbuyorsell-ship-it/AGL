# Logbook and records review

The records are the aircraft's history and a large part of its value. A thorough review often finds more than the physical inspection. Treat missing records as a finding, not a detail.

## Records to request

- Airframe logbooks from new
- Engine logbooks from new, including the overhaul records and work orders
- Propeller logbooks from new
- Avionics and appliance records where kept separately
- Major repair and alteration records: FAA Form 337 for US aircraft, or equivalent repair and modification approvals elsewhere
- STC documents and the associated flight manual supplements and instructions for continued airworthiness (ICA)
- Current weight and balance report and equipment list
- AD compliance record or status list for airframe, engine, propeller and appliances
- Life-limited and calendar-limited component status
- Certificate of airworthiness, registration certificate and, under EASA, the ARC and the aircraft maintenance programme (AMP)
- Pilot's Operating Handbook or Aircraft Flight Manual matching the serial number
- Recent oil analysis reports, borescope images and compression test results, if any

Ask whether all logbooks back to new are present. If not, ask which periods are missing and why.

## Review checklist

### Continuity

- Hours and dates progress in order across all logbooks. Tachometer or Hobbs readings never go backwards without an explained instrument change.
- No unexplained gaps in time or dates. A long gap may mean storage (corrosion risk), lost logs, or an event the seller did not record.
- Engine and propeller serial numbers in the logs match the data plates and the airframe log.
- Each inspection entry identifies the person who signed it and their certificate or licence number. Entries look consistent, not like a reconstruction in one hand and one pen.
- Time recorded at each annual or 100-hour inspection is consistent with the hours flown in between.

### Inspection status

- Date and type of the last annual, 100-hour, phase or ARC inspection, and who performed it
- Whether the aircraft was used for hire or training, which brings additional inspection requirements
- Recurring inspections from ADs, SBs or the maintenance programme, with the next due time or date
- Avionics certification items: in the US, the altimeter and static system check (required for instrument flight) and the transponder check, each every 24 calendar months; the ELT inspection and battery dates

### Airworthiness Directives

- A complete AD status list covering airframe, engine, propeller and appliances such as magnetos, vacuum pumps, ELTs and seat rails
- For each AD: number and revision, method of compliance, date and time of compliance, and next due for recurring ADs
- Spot-check that the logbook entries support the status list
- Tell the user that the list must be checked against the official AD database for the actual serial numbers; you cannot confirm applicability yourself

### Engine

- Time since new and since major overhaul (SMOH) or factory rebuild (SFRM). A rebuilt engine from the factory has zero time; a field overhaul does not reset total time since new.
- Overhaul shop, type of overhaul and the parts used: new, overhauled, serviceable
- Calendar time since overhaul against the manufacturer's calendar recommendation, typically 12 years for common Lycoming and Continental piston engines; confirm the current service instruction for the model
- Top overhauls or cylinder replacements: when, why and how many
- Oil consumption history, oil analysis trends and filter inspection findings
- Any prop strike, sudden stoppage or overspeed, and whether the required teardown inspection was performed per the manufacturer's service bulletin
- For turbines: cycles, hot section inspection, trend monitoring data and whether the engine is on an hourly maintenance programme and that programme is transferable

### Propeller

- Hours and calendar time since overhaul against the manufacturer's recommendation, which often has both an hour and a calendar limit
- Any strike, blade damage or blending record
- Governor overhaul status for constant-speed and turbine propellers

### Airframe

- Corrosion findings and treatment history, especially on aircraft based near the sea or stored outdoors
- Major repairs: what, where on the airframe, who approved them and whether they were done to approved data
- Major alterations and STCs: each installed modification should have its paperwork, its flight manual supplement and, where required, an updated weight and balance
- Life-limited components, such as some seat-belt airbag systems, certain control system parts and helicopter dynamic components, which are often tightly time-limited
- Landing gear: damage, gear-up landings and the related repair records

### Damage history

Look for entries that mention structural repair, skin replacement, wing or tail removal and reinstallation, landing gear replacement, firewall work or propeller replacement out of schedule. Any of these may indicate an accident or incident. Recommend checking public accident databases such as NTSB CAROL in the US or the national investigation body in Europe for the registration and serial number.

## Common abbreviations

| Abbreviation | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| TT, TTAF | Total time airframe |
| SMOH | Since major overhaul |
| SFRM | Since factory remanufacture or rebuild |
| STOH, SPOH | Since top overhaul, since propeller overhaul |
| TSN, CSN | Time and cycles since new (turbines) |
| HSI | Hot section inspection (turbines) |
| TBO | Time between overhauls, as recommended by the manufacturer |
| AD | Airworthiness Directive (mandatory) |
| SB, SI, SL | Service Bulletin, Service Instruction, Service Letter (manufacturer guidance) |
| STC | Supplemental Type Certificate |
| ICA | Instructions for Continued Airworthiness |
| ARC | Airworthiness Review Certificate (EASA) |
| AMP | Aircraft Maintenance Programme (EASA) |

## Output

Summarise the records review as a table of areas with status (Complete, Gap, Concern, Unknown) and a list of findings graded Stop, Major, Minor or Info.
